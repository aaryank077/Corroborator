    Concept project · Product design

    Knowing what you know during breaking news

    Corroborate is a workspace for a newsroom's breaking-news desk. It turns a flood of posts, alerts and calls into a short list of claims, shows how many truly independent sources stand behind each one, and keeps the published story tied to that evidence.

    The problem

    When something big breaks, a newsroom is flooded with claims within minutes: social posts, wire alerts, scanner traffic, reporters' calls, tips and press releases. The editor running the story has to decide, again and again, what can be published now, how to attribute it, and what has to wait.

    That work usually happens across a chat channel, a shared document and people's memory. The same failures come up over and over:

    • Duplicate checking. Two reporters call the same official because neither knew the other was on it.
    • False corroboration. A detail looks confirmed because three outlets have it, but all three are repeating one original report.
    • Orphaned errors. A detail is disproved, but it stays in the live story because nobody connected the two.

    The design question: how might an editor see, for every claim, how much independent evidence stands behind it, what's still missing, and where it already appears in print?

    Who it's for

    Breaking-news editor

    Primary user

    Runs the live story. Decides what goes in, how it's attributed, and when to correct.

    Reporter in the field

    Brings in sources

    Makes calls and gets to the scene. Needs to know what to chase and what's already handled.

    Verification specialist

    Checks media and accounts

    Traces images and video back to where they first appeared. Needs a fast way to flag a fake.

    Standards editor

    Reviews afterward

    Cares about attribution and corrections. Needs a record of why each line was published.

    Research

    I've worked as a journalist, so I know the desk from the inside. To see how a major breaking story unfolds across outlets, minute by minute, I went to the public record.

    1. Reconstructing a real breaking story

    On 2 January 2024 a Japan Airlines jet collided with a Coast Guard plane while landing at Tokyo's Haneda Airport. All 379 people on the airliner got out; five of the six Coast Guard crew died. Using archived copies of NHK's Japanese coverage (the root source for most early facts), CNN's live blog and AP's story from the Wayback Machine, I traced ten claims through the first day: where each came from, how it changed, and whether it held up against the investigators' later findings.

    One source, many outlets. Every casualty figure in CNN's first hour of crash coverage cited one broadcaster, NHK, and AP's first version leaned on NHK too. Outlets that seemed to agree were relaying the same report.
    Copies freeze a moving number. NHK's injury count went from 1 to 17 to 14 over the evening. CNN kept 17 and AP used 14, so each froze a different moment of the same source. One CNN post also gave 347 passengers instead of 367.
    Official isn't final. JAL's own timeline put the evacuation at about 20 minutes. Investigators later found it took 11.
    Each outlet quoted half an answer. Asked whether the jet was cleared to land, JAL said it couldn't say yet, then that it would normally assume so. NHK ran the whole answer. CNN quoted the first half, AP the second.
    Translation shifts meaning. NHK reported the Coast Guard captain as 重傷, "seriously injured," which proved right. In CNN's English it became "critical condition." An acceptable imprecision here, but a reason to keep the original wording beside any translation.
    Summaries lose sources. A CNN roundup merged an NHK figure and a JAL figure into one sentence with no attribution.
    Holding back works. Both outlets called the Coast Guard crew "missing" until police or the transport minister confirmed the deaths.

    5. Usability tests

    Five people, about 15 minutes each, thinking aloud. They don't need newsroom experience: the tests check whether the screen is clear, not whether the journalism is right.

    1. Three sources report a worker was killed. Can it be published? Measures whether people notice that all three trace back to one broadcast.
    2. The county coroner confirms the death on the record. Log it and publish. Measures time and errors.
    3. A video in the live story turns out to be from another fire. Fix the story. Measures whether the correction is found and completed.
    4. A county briefing says three workers were hospitalized; the story says two. Update it. Measures whether the number check is noticed and used.

    What this research can't tell me

    Archives show what was published, not how decisions were made inside the newsroom. I compared two English-language outlets against NHK's Japanese coverage, the only Japanese outlet I checked in depth. One event is a case, not a pattern; the corrections study is meant to test whether these findings generalize.

    Design decisions

    Each decision names the alternative I considered and why I didn't take it.

    Organize by claim, not by feed
    Chose
    Posts and alerts are grouped into claims. Sixty-three posts about a death become one claim with sixty-three mentions.
    Instead of
    A faster, better-filtered feed.
    Because
    The editor's decision is about a claim. A feed makes them repeat the same judgment every time a new post appears.
    Count independent sources, not reports
    Chose
    Every source records what it's based on. Reports that repeat another report are nested under it and don't add to the count.
    Instead of
    A simple source count, or a confidence score.
    Because
    Three outlets citing one broadcast is one source. A plain count would call that corroborated. A score would hide the reason.
    Say what's missing, not only what's wrong
    Chose
    Each claim states what it needs to meet the newsroom's standard, such as "one more independent source, or an official on the record."
    Instead of
    A status label alone.
    Because
    "Unverified" doesn't tell a reporter what to do next. The missing piece is the next assignment.
    Show who's checking what
    Chose
    Each claim shows who has it and what they're doing, and anyone can take an unassigned claim in one click.
    Instead of
    Assignments in the chat channel.
    Because
    Assignments in chat scroll away within minutes, and that's how two people end up calling the same official.
    Link every published line to its claim
    Chose
    When a claim is marked false, every line that depends on it is flagged in the live story, with a button to correct it.
    Instead of
    Relying on the editor to remember.
    Because
    Corrections are where a newsroom's credibility is won or lost, and memory is the weakest link during breaking news.
    Status by shape as well as color
    Chose
    Each verification level has its own glyph: dotted, half-filled, ringed, solid, hatched.
    Instead of
    Color alone.
    Because
    The glyphs still read for people with color-vision deficiency and on a dim second monitor at 2 a.m.

    What the research changed

    Four changes came directly out of the Haneda reconstruction. Each is in the prototype.

    "Confirmed" can expire
    Evidence
    JAL's own evacuation time was later replaced by the investigators' figure, and injury counts changed through the day. Neither was false when published.
    Change
    A new Superseded status, separate from False. Confirmation now says who confirmed it and as of when. The old line stays in the story, struck through.
    Try it
    C11, a county briefing that says three workers were hospitalized.
    Check numbers across the whole story
    Evidence
    One CNN page carried 367 and 347 passengers at the same time.
    Change
    Lines that give the same quantity different values are flagged, and publishing a new figure offers to supersede the old line.
    Try it
    Publish C11 either way and watch the live story.
    Keep the source's exact words
    Evidence
    JAL's answer on landing clearance held both a refusal and an assumption; CNN and AP each kept one half. Separately, "seriously injured" became "critical" in translation.
    Change
    Each source can carry an exact quote, shown under it, separate from the reporter's note.
    Try it
    C10: "accounted for" doesn't mean unhurt, which matters next to C3 and C11.
    Summaries keep their sources
    Evidence
    A roundup merged figures from two sources into one unattributed sentence.
    Change
    The "What we know" box at the top of the live story lists each point with its source, built from the claims themselves.
    Try it
    Publish or supersede anything and the summary updates.

    Designing for the overloaded hour

    A breaking story doesn't arrive as ten tidy claims. The prototype now carries 150, most of them weak: one post, no source, nobody assigned. The interface has to make the handful that need a decision impossible to miss, and let an editor clear the rest quickly.

    Filters before folders
    Chose
    Search plus five filter chips, each showing a live count (All, Needs decision, Unassigned, Mine, Conflicts), and a switch to group by stage, topic or owner.
    Instead of
    Fixed folders, or one long list sorted by time.
    Because
    The editor's question changes minute to minute: "what's ready?", then "what's nobody on?", then "what about the evacuation?" The counts answer the question before the click.
    Density on demand
    Chose
    A compact mode that collapses each row to one line, so about three times as many claims fit on screen.
    Instead of
    One fixed row height.
    Because
    Scanning 150 items and reading one carefully are different tasks, and the same person does both within the same minute.
    Bulk triage, with undo
    Chose
    Checkboxes (or x) to select many claims, then assign them to someone or dismiss them in one step. Dismissed claims stay searchable and can be reopened.
    Instead of
    Handling claims one at a time.
    Because
    Most of the long tail is noise. Clearing it fast is what keeps the important claims visible.
    Keyboard first
    Chose
    j/k to move, / to search, x to select, m for the source map, ? for the full list.
    Instead of
    Mouse only.
    Because
    Desk editors live in this screen for hours. Expert speed comes from the keyboard.

    The source map

    A list of sources per claim can't show the most dangerous pattern from the Haneda research: one origin quietly sitting behind several claims. The source map lays every claim under active coverage out in three columns (original sources, the reports that repeat them, and the claims they support) and outlines in amber any origin that feeds more than one claim.

    In the prototype, Channel 8's unnamed "source close to the investigation" is behind both the reported death (C4) and the detail that the worker was a night-shift supervisor (C12). Each claim on its own looks like a routine single-source claim. The map shows they stand or fall together, and the claim view now warns about it too.

    A layered map, not a free-form graph
    Chose
    Fixed columns read left to right, with the selected claim's whole lineage highlighted and everything else dimmed.
    Instead of
    A force-directed network diagram.
    Because
    Free-form graphs look impressive but move every time data changes, so nothing stays where the editor last saw it. Columns keep each item in a stable place and match the question being asked: where did this come from?

    Engineering tradeoffs

    How the design would meet a real system, and what I'd cut to ship a first version.

    Mentiona post, alert or tip
    grouped into →
    Claimone checkable statement
    ← supported by
    Sourcea person, record or report
    ↻ repeats
    Story linerelies on a claim
    • Grouping posts into claims is the hardest part to automate. Version 1 would suggest groups and let the editor confirm them, rather than trusting automatic clustering on a story where a wrong merge could publish an error.
    • Lineage depends on reporters filling in "Based on." To keep that cheap, it defaults to Independent and offers the claim's existing sources as one-click choices. The source map is computed from those fields and is never drawn by hand.
    • The number check needs structured numbers. Version 1 checks only the counts editors enter themselves (injured, hospitalized, dead). Pulling numbers out of free text would come later.
    • Several editors work at once. Every change would sync live, and a conflicting edit would show who changed what and when, instead of silently overwriting it.
    • Cut from version 1: built-in reverse image search, a phone view for reporters, automatic clustering, and an after-action review mode.

    The verification ladder

    Iterations

    Five rounds so far, each set off by something I learned. The sixth is still to come.

    Round 0Choosing the problem

    I started with a different project: a bed-placement console for hospital coordinators. The interface held up, but the research didn't. I had no way to reach the people who do that job, and desk research couldn't check the clinical rules the design depended on. So I switched to breaking news, a field I know, with a deep public record.

    Learned: pick a problem where you can get evidence, not just one that looks good on screen.

    Round 1First prototype

    Built around three ideas: group posts into claims, count independent sources rather than reports, and say what each claim still needs. Five statuses, from Unverified to False.

    UnverifiedSingle sourceCorroboratedConfirmedFalse

    Assumed: a claim moves up the ladder until it's confirmed, or it's shown to be false.

    Round 2After reconstructing Haneda

    The CNN and AP archives broke that assumption. Figures that were confirmed when published, like JAL's evacuation time and the injury count, were later replaced without ever being false. And the same live page carried two different passenger counts. Four changes followed. The clearest two are below, drawn with the prototype's own components.

    The other two changes: a Superseded status with "confirmed by whom, as of when," and a "What we know" summary in which every point keeps its source.

    Learned: "true or false" was the wrong model for a live story. Most problems were facts that went out of date.

    Round 3After checking NHK's originals

    Reading NHK's Japanese coverage, the root source, sharpened two findings. JAL's answer on landing clearance contained both a refusal and an assumption, and CNN and AP each quoted one half. That made the case for storing exact words much stronger than my first reading of it. NHK also called the Coast Guard captain 重傷, "seriously injured," which became "critical condition" in English.

    NextKeep a source's original-language wording beside the translation, so a shift like 重傷 → "critical" is visible to the editor. Not built yet.

    Learned: go back to the root source. Two English outlets agreeing told me less than one Japanese page.

    Round 4Stress-testing at scale

    Eleven hand-picked claims made the design look calm. Loading 150 realistic ones showed what that hid: the stage groups pushed the few claims that needed a decision below the fold, and per-claim source lists couldn't show one origin feeding several claims. So I added filters with counts, compact rows, bulk triage, the source map, and designed states for an offline feed and a reporter's permissions.

    Learned: test the design against the worst hour, not the demo.

    How I'd know it works

    • Time to publish: minutes from a claim's first report to publication, for claims that meet the standard.
    • Duplicate checks: claims worked on by more than one person without knowing it.
    • Time to correct: minutes from a claim being marked false to the published fix.
    • Traceability: share of published lines that can be traced to a logged source.

    What's next

    • A phone view for reporters in the field to log sources and see their assignments.
    • Automatic grouping of posts into claims, with the editor confirming each group.
    • Reverse image search built into the verification workflow.
    • An after-action review mode for the standards editor.
